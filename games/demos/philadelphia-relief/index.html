    Philadelphia, in relief

    Real elevation for Philadelphia and its suburbs, with 14,500 real building footprints standing on it — the Center City skyline, the rowhouse grid and the Delaware bridges. The land here is subtle, about 380 m of relief across 94 km, so the vertical scale is exaggerated to make it legible.

    • Drag to move across the region
    • Right-drag or two fingers to orbit and tilt
    • Scroll or pinch to zoom
    • Press ▶ for a captioned tour — moving the camera pauses it
    • Pull back with the Delaware Valley preset for the whole region; the bridge chips fly to each crossing

    About this map

    A cinematic relief map of the Philadelphia region — Center City, Port Richmond, Northeast and Northwest Philadelphia, the Main Line, and Delaware, Montgomery, Chester and Bucks Counties, across the river into Camden, Cherry Hill and the Burlington County plain.

    Where the data comes from

    Elevation
    AWS Terrain Tiles (Mapzen terrarium encoding), sampled at zoom 12 and resampled to a 2048 × 2048 grid — about 46 m per sample. The underlying elevation for this region is USGS 3DEP and SRTM.
    Rivers, roads, rail, boundaries, parks, place names
    © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL 1.0. Queried once from the public Overpass API at build time and baked into static files — the page makes no third-party requests at runtime.
    Landmarks
    Hand-curated for this map. Elevations are sampled from the same DEM as the ground.
    Buildings
    — OpenStreetMap footprints in two detail zones: every building in Center City and University City, and only the notable ones (tall, large or named) across inner Philadelphia and Camden. — carry a measured OSM height, most of it from the City of Philadelphia's LiDAR-derived footprint import; — are estimated from storey counts or building type; — skyline towers with no height in OSM (One Liberty Place, Comcast Center and their neighbours) use public reference heights supplied by this project, plus the City Hall tower as a curated box.
    Bridges
    — Delaware crossings — —. Deck alignment and width come from OpenStreetMap; spans, tower heights and clearances are rounded public reference values, and the towers, cables, trusses and arches are schematic forms built from them, not surveys of the steel.

    What this map is not

    This is a visualisation, not a survey. Elevations come from a ~30 m public DEM resampled to ~46 m, then vertically exaggerated by whatever the slider says — the default 10× is not a real slope. Buildings and bridges are stretched by the square root of that factor (times the structure-height slider), so a tower stays proportionate to a hill without becoming a needle; their footprints are simplified to ~0.6 m and rowhouse rows are merged into single blocks. Readouts are good to a few metres at best. Three source-data seams in the northwest (thin stripes spiking to 900 m in ground that is really ~260 m) were replaced with a local median during the build. Simplified vectors drop detail below roughly 25–140 m depending on the layer, minor roads and unnamed streams are not included at all, and OpenStreetMap coverage varies by area. Do not use any of this for navigation, flood risk, engineering or property decisions.

    How it is built

    Three.js on WebGL 2. The terrain is one draw call: a grid that is log-warped around the camera target in the vertex shader, so detail concentrates where you are looking without any LOD seams. Hillshade, contours, ambient occlusion and the atmosphere are computed per pixel from the height field. Buildings are one draw call per zone and height tier, packed tallest-first so the density slider is a draw-range prefix; whole tiers frustum-cull as a unit and rise out of the ground as you approach. Every byte is served from this origin — no map service, no account, no API key, nothing to leak.
