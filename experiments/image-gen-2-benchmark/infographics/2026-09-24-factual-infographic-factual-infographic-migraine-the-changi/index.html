Migraine — The Changing Shape of an Attack

Source-verified medical anatomy and clinical infographic on variable migraine phases, aura, symptoms, diagnosis and individualized management.

Research behind the infographic

  • Migraine is a neurologic health condition with recurrent episodes and symptoms beyond head pain. [1]
  • A typical episode can include prodrome, aura in some people, headache, and postdrome; phases and their order vary, and not every person has every phase. [1] [2]
  • Prodrome may feature yawning, mood change, fatigue, or food cravings before head pain. [1] [2]
  • Aura occurs only in some people and may include evolving zigzags or bright spots, tingling, or language difficulty; aura is not required for migraine. [1] [3]
  • Migraine head pain is often throbbing and one-sided but can vary; nausea and sensitivity to light, sound, and smell can accompany it. [1] [2]
  • Postdrome can include fatigue, dizziness, and difficulty concentrating after headache subsides. [1] [2]
  • Migraine-related head pain involves interacting nerve signaling, brain chemicals, and blood vessels; CGRP is a protein involved in pain signaling. [1]
  • Possible triggers vary by person and may include menstrual hormone changes, disrupted sleep, or changes in routine; a trigger is not a universal cause. [1]
  • A headache diary of onset, duration, associated symptoms, sleep, and medicines can help a clinician recognize patterns and guide care. [1] [2]
  • Clinicians diagnose migraine mainly from history and physical or neurologic examination; imaging may be used if another condition is suspected, not routinely to prove migraine. [1]
  • Acute care may include resting in a quiet dark room and clinician-selected medicines such as triptans or CGRP-targeting agents; treatment choice is individual. [1]
  • Preventive care can involve regular sleep and meals, hydration, exercise, and clinician-selected preventive medication for suitable patients. [1]
  • Sudden-onset or persistent new aura-like symptoms, one-sided weakness, or altered alertness warrant prompt medical evaluation rather than assuming migraine. [3]
