Inguinal Hernia — Abdominal Wall Passage

Source-backed clinical anatomy, symptoms, assessment, management and urgent complications of inguinal hernia.

Research behind the infographic

  • An inguinal hernia is abdominal contents bulging through a weak area of the lower abdominal wall at the groin; contents often include fat or small intestine. [1]
  • The peritoneum forms a sac around the hernia; inguinal canals are passages through the lower abdominal wall. [1]
  • Indirect hernias relate to an opening present at birth; direct hernias relate to wall weakness developing later. [1]
  • Groin bulge, aching, burning or heaviness may worsen with coughing, straining, lifting or prolonged standing and improve lying down. [1] [2]
  • Clinical history and examination are used for diagnosis; imaging may help when the diagnosis is unclear. [1] [2]
  • Most patients need surgical repair; open or laparoscopic repair may use mesh to reinforce the weak area. [1]
  • Watchful waiting may be appropriate for some men with few or no symptoms, with regular medical follow-up; this is not advice for everyone. [1]
  • Incarceration means trapped contents; strangulation means blood supply is cut off and requires emergency care. [1]
  • Seek urgent medical help for sudden severe pain, a suddenly larger or newly irreducible bulge, fever, redness, nausea or vomiting; obstruction may cause bloating. [1]
  • Family history and aging are associated with increased risk; chronic cough, constipation and repeated heavy lifting may contribute through increased abdominal pressure. [1]
