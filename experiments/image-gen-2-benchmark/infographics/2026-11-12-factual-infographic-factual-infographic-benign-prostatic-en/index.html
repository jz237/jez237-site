Benign Prostatic Enlargement — When Bladder Outflow Narrows

Anatomical explanation of benign prostatic enlargement, urinary symptoms, clinical assessment, management and urgent warning signs.

Research behind the infographic

  • BPH is noncancerous enlargement of the prostate; it becomes more common with age. Its cause is uncertain; age-related hormonal changes may contribute. [1] [2]
  • The prostate lies below the bladder and surrounds the urethra at the bladder neck; enlargement can squeeze the urethra and impede urine flow. [1] [2]
  • The bladder works harder against narrowed outflow and may eventually fail to empty fully, leaving residual urine. [1]
  • Symptoms can include difficulty starting, weak or interrupted stream, incomplete emptying, frequency, urgency and nighttime urination. [1] [2]
  • Prostate size does not reliably predict symptom severity; other urinary disorders can cause similar symptoms and require evaluation. [1]
  • Evaluation includes history, examination and urine tests; selected patients may need PSA testing, urine-flow testing or bladder ultrasound. [1] [2]
  • Mild symptoms may be monitored; reducing caffeine and alcohol and shifting fluids away from bedtime while maintaining daytime hydration may help. [1] [2]
  • Clinician-selected alpha blockers relax muscles in the prostate and bladder neck; 5-alpha reductase inhibitors can help shrink the prostate. Procedures may relieve obstruction when symptoms are severe or medicines fail. Treatment has benefits and risks and is individualized. [1] [2]
  • Seek urgent medical care if unable to urinate, for blood in urine, or for painful urgent urination with fever and chills or severe lower abdominal pain. [1] [2]
