Burying Beetle — A Nursery Beneath the Soil

Species-specific carrion preparation, social immunity and facultative parental provisioning in a burying beetle.

Research behind the infographic

  • Nicrophorus vespilloides is a real burying beetle in the genus Nicrophorus, family Silphidae. [1] [3]
  • The adult specimen has a black head and pronotum, black clubbed antennae, six jointed legs, and orange transverse markings on dark, shortened elytra with the abdominal tip exposed; the reference photo supplies diagnostic visual anatomy. [3]
  • N. vespilloides occurs in woodland populations west of Cambridge, England; the illustrated leaf litter and soil are a representative woodland context, not a full-range map. [4]
  • Adults locate small vertebrate carrion, which serves as the breeding resource and food for offspring; adults use flight in locating carrion. [1] [5]
  • Parents remove fur or feathers, roll the flesh into a ball and bury the prepared carcass in an underground chamber. [1]
  • Parents smear the carcass with exudates that inhibit microbial growth; this is social immunity, not complete sterilization. [2]
  • Lysozymes are antimicrobial enzymes implicated in social immunity; female anal-exudate antimicrobial properties correlate with expression of a particular lysozyme gene. No specific molecular cutting reaction is illustrated. [2]
  • After hatching, larvae feed on the prepared carcass and can also receive regurgitated meat from parents; mouth-to-mouth feeding is depicted as provisioning, not attack. [1]
  • Biparental and single-parent care occur in this species; the illustrated pair is one possible family arrangement, not an obligation. [1] [5]
  • Larvae disperse from the carrion for pupation and later emerge as adults; the life-history strip is schematic, not to scale and gives no fixed timing or brood size. [1] [5]
  • Carrion is a scarce resource and beetles compete over it; mandibles are used in contests, with larger individuals often advantaged, not guaranteed winners. [4] [5]
  • The beetles transform vertebrate carrion into food for growing offspring, an ecological carrion-use relationship; no broader quantified recycling benefit is claimed. [1] [5]
