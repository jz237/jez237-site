Meadow Spittlebug — A Nymph Inside a Bubble Shelter

Macro scientific plate of nymph foam production with seven biological studies and a clearly qualified generalized digestive cutaway.

Research behind the infographic

  • The meadow spittlebug, Philaenus spumarius, is a hemipteran; its adult is also called a froghopper. [1] [2]
  • Meadow spittlebug nymphs are pale green or yellow; lime-green nymphs can have red eyes. Adults are generally tan to brown or gray. [1] [2]
  • The meadow spittlebug occurs in the North American Midwest and feeds on many herbaceous plants including clover and strawberries. [1]
  • Both adults and nymphs feed on water-carrying xylem sap; its low nutrient concentration requires processing large quantities. [1]
  • Hemipteran piercing-sucking mouthparts extend below the head; hoppers have a down-and-back-pointing proboscis. A simplified feeding study can show mouthparts reaching plant sap tissues. [4] [1]
  • Nymphs feed head downward and excrete excess sap through the anus, not the mouth. [1]
  • Epidermal gland secretions stabilize foam; air is mixed with excreted fluid as abdominal movements form bubbles. [1] [2]
  • Nymphs use their legs to pull forming bubbles forward over the body. [1] [2]
  • The foam conceals nymphs from predators, buffers temperature extremes and helps prevent drying. It is protective, not an invulnerable shield. [1] [2]
  • In the described temperate life cycle eggs overwinter in plant stems, nymphs hatch in spring, molt as they grow, and become winged adults; females lay eggs in late summer to fall. There is no pupal stage in hemipteran incomplete metamorphosis. [1] [4]
  • Adult spittlebugs are tiny, under a quarter inch long, and hop between plants; adult wings must not appear on the young nymph. [1] [2] [4]
  • General insect digestive anatomy has a tube-like alimentary canal running mouth to anus with foregut, midgut and hindgut; midgut nutrient absorption is a generalized function, not a species-specific measured dissection. [3]
  • The insect body plan has six legs and distinct head, thorax and abdomen; small wingless hemipteran nymphs differ from winged adults. [4]
