Celiac Disease — The Intestinal Surface

An artwork-led clinical atlas of celiac disease, normal versus damaged villi, variable symptoms, testing before dietary change, and evidence-based management.

Research behind the infographic

  • Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition: eating gluten triggers an abnormal immune reaction that damages the small intestine. [1] [2] [4]
  • Gluten occurs in wheat, barley and rye. [1] [2] [4]
  • Villi are projections of the small-intestinal lining that increase surface area; inflammation and damage can flatten them and impair nutrient absorption. Normal and celiac-damaged villi are schematic comparisons, not before-and-after images from a patient. [2] [3]
  • Symptoms may include bloating, abdominal pain, diarrhea and fatigue; some people have no symptoms. [1] [2]
  • Nutrient malabsorption can lead to iron-deficiency anemia and weakening of bones. [1] [2] [4]
  • Celiac disease has genetic susceptibility and increased risk in close relatives; eating gluten alone does not mean someone will develop it. [1] [2]
  • Evaluation commonly uses antibody blood tests and small-intestinal biopsy; symptoms alone do not diagnose it. Do not start a gluten-free diet before diagnostic testing without medical advice because this can affect results. [1]
  • Treatment is a lifelong gluten-free diet with dietitian support; for most people it improves symptoms and allows intestinal healing, but is not a cure. [1] [2] [4]
  • Read food labels, avoid hidden gluten, and follow up with a clinician to assess symptoms and nutritional deficiencies and supplement only if needed. [4]
  • Persistent symptoms despite a gluten-free diet warrant clinical reassessment. [4]
  • With diarrhea or vomiting, inability to keep fluids down or bloody diarrhea requires urgent advice; sudden severe abdominal pain requires emergency care. These are general warning signs, not diagnostic signs of celiac disease. [5]
  • Celiac disease differs from wheat allergy and non-celiac gluten sensitivity. [1]
