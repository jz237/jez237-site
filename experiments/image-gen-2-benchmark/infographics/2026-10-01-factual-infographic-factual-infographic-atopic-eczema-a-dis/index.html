Atopic Eczema — A Disrupted Skin Barrier

Source-backed clinical infographic on atopic dermatitis, barrier dysfunction, variable symptoms, assessment, management and infection warning signs.

Research behind the infographic

  • Atopic dermatitis is a chronic inflammatory skin disease, often starting in childhood but possible at any age; it is not contagious. [1] [2]
  • Barrier changes allow moisture to escape and expose the skin immune system to the environment; genes, immune activity and environmental factors contribute, and there is no single known cause. [1]
  • Skin has a protective outer layer; a schematic intact-versus-disrupted surface comparison illustrates moisture retention versus increased water loss, not a literal hole through the whole skin. [1]
  • Inflammation can trigger itch; scratching causes further skin damage and inflammation and increases bacterial infection risk. [1] [3]
  • Symptoms include intense itching, dryness, cracking, scaling and thickened skin; scratches may bleed or weep clear fluid. [1] [2]
  • Eczema appearance varies with skin tone: it can appear red, purple, grey or lighter or darker than surrounding skin; inflammation may leave color changes. [1] [2]
  • Common sites include elbow and knee bends and hands; babies often have facial eczema. Distribution varies by age and can occur elsewhere. [1] [2]
  • Symptoms fluctuate between flares and improvement; itch can disturb sleep. [1] [2]
  • Family history of eczema, asthma or hay fever is associated with increased likelihood, not proof of one cause. [1] [2]
  • Soap, detergents, fragrances, rough fabrics, heat and stress may worsen symptoms; triggers vary by person. [1] [2] [3]
  • Diagnosis involves clinical skin examination and medical, family and trigger history; clinicians may use tests to rule out other causes. [3]
  • Regular moisturizing, including immediately after bathing, helps retain water and supports the skin barrier; gentle unscented or non-soap cleansing and lukewarm water can help. [2] [3]
  • Avoid personal irritants, keep cool and keep nails short to reduce scratching damage; do not change diet unless advised by a clinician. [2] [3]
  • Topical corticosteroids reduce inflammation; other topical treatments, phototherapy or systemic medicines may be considered by clinicians depending on severity, age and response, not as universal treatments. [2] [3]
  • Treatment can control symptoms but there is currently no cure; seek care for symptoms or treatments that are not helping. [1] [2]
  • Painful warm swollen skin, pus, sudden worsening or spreading, fever or feeling unwell, and new blisters or leaking/crusting can signal infection or eczema herpeticum and warrant urgent medical advice. [2]
  • Emollients transferred onto clothing, bedding or dressings can make fabrics catch fire more easily; avoid smoking and naked flames. [2]
