Lichens — Two Kingdoms, One Body

How lichens form a fungus-photobiont partnership with a layered thallus, three growth forms and dual reproduction

Research behind the infographic

  • A lichen is a stable symbiotic partnership between a fungus and a photosynthetic partner (photobiont): green algae and/or cyanobacteria. [1] [3]
  • The photobiont photosynthesizes and supplies sugars (carbon) to the fungus; the partnership is thought to be a mutualism. [1] [3]
  • The fungus is the dominant partner and gives the lichen most of its characteristics, including thallus shape and fruiting bodies. [1]
  • About 90% of lichens use a green-algal photobiont; some lichens have both green algae and cyanobacteria. [1] [3]
  • Lichens have no roots, stems, leaves, waxy cuticle or vascular tissue; they absorb water and nutrients from air and rain. [1] [2]
  • The thallus is layered: an outer cortex of tightly packed fungal cells, an algal layer beneath it, and a medulla of loosely packed threadlike fungal filaments. [2]
  • When a lichen is wet, cortex cells become transparent and the algal layer's color shows through; green algae often make a wet lichen bright green. [2]
  • Rhizines are fungal filaments from the medulla that attach the lichen to its surface; they do not move water or nutrients like roots. [2]
  • Some foliose lichens attach by one central holdfast (umbilicate lichens, such as rock tripe). [2]
  • Three main growth forms: crustose (pressed against the surface like a crust), foliose (leafy, with distinct upper and lower sides), fruticose (hair-like, shrubby or cup-like). [2] [3]
  • The fungus produces spores from fruiting bodies; a new fungus must find a compatible photobiont partner. [2]
  • Lichens also reproduce vegetatively: soredia are granules of algae and fungi intertwined for dispersal, isidia are vegetative outgrowths, and fragments can grow into new thalli. [2]
  • By protecting its photobiont, the fungus lets lichens live in dry, sunny places where algae alone could not, recharging with occasional rain. [1]
  • Lichens absorb pollutants such as heavy metals and sulfur from the air, so scientists use them to monitor air quality; old man's beard (Usnea longissima) is an air-pollution indicator. [1] [2]
  • Lichens are not mosses: mosses are plants with leaf-like structures, while lichens are not plants. [1]
