Anna's Hummingbird — A Feather Built to Flash

A direct-canvas museum infographic tracing light from a male Anna's hummingbird gorget through feather barbules and their keratin, melanosome, and air multilayers to explain directional, angle-dependent structural color.

Research behind the infographic

  • The male Anna's hummingbird is the real species Calypte anna, and its crown and gorget include bright reddish-pink iridescent feathers. [2]
  • A feather branches from rachis to barbs to smaller barbules; the iridescent optical structures are located within the barbules. [1] [2]
  • The iridescent barbules contain stacks of melanin-bearing melanosomes embedded in and separated by keratin, with internal air spaces that create strong refractive-index contrasts. [1] [2]
  • These repeating melanosome, keratin, and air layers act as an optical multilayer whose reflections reinforce selected wavelengths and produce structural color. [1] [2]
  • The reflected color and brightness depend strongly on illumination and viewing angle, so a feather can flash brightly on-angle and become much darker when rotated off-angle. [1] [2]
  • In measured Anna's hummingbird feathers, changing the angle of incidence shifted the reflected color toward shorter wavelengths, behavior characteristic of an optical multilayer. [2]
  • The distal portion of an Anna's hummingbird gorget feather carries the strongest reddish-pink reflection, while the reflecting barbules are oriented to produce a directional, approximately specular glint. [2]
  • Hummingbirds vary in melanosome shape, stacking, air content, and feather-layer dimensions, helping produce diverse iridescent colors; not every hummingbird feather is brightly structural. [1]
  • The infographic's comparison between pigment-only absorption and structural reflection is qualitative: melanin contributes to the optical system, but nanoscale organization is essential to the iridescent effect. [1] [2]
