Phonophobia — Persistent Fear of Sound

An artwork-led educational chart of fear of sound, its triggers and bodily response, clinical distinctions and professionally guided help.

Research behind the infographic

  • Phonophobia is an established clinical term for persistent fear of sound; here it means fear of sound, not a separately named diagnostic category. [1]
  • Environmental sounds such as traffic, closing doors and loud speech can trigger fear, even at non-damaging levels. [1]
  • Anticipation of feared sounds and learned reactions can accompany avoidance of sound-related situations. [1]
  • Autonomic arousal can include palpitations, sweating and trembling. [1] [2]
  • Avoidance may restrict social participation and daily life. [1]
  • Fear is not a measurement of acoustic danger; non-damaging sounds can trigger fear. Genuine hazardous noise should not be used for exposure. [1]
  • Phonophobia and hyperacusis are not interchangeable; hearing-related and emotional components can overlap and require clinical assessment. [1]
  • In migraine literature phonophobia can mean sound intolerance rather than a phobic disorder. [1]
  • CBT is an evidence-based talking therapy used for phobias; professionally guided graded exposure may be part of treatment, with an individualized safe plan rather than forced loud-sound exposure. [1] [2]
  • Seek a clinician if fear affects daily life; painful sound or hearing concerns merit hearing assessment. General education is not diagnosis. [1] [2]
