Water Scorpion — Breathing Through a Tail Tube

Detailed macro scientific plate of the water scorpion, paired siphon respiration and predatory biology, with explicitly generalized internal breathing anatomy.

Research behind the infographic

  • The water scorpion is Nepa cinerea, an insect in Hemiptera, family Nepidae, not a true scorpion. [1] [4]
  • Its flattened body hides among submerged vegetation and dead leaves; it ambushes aquatic insects and small animals including tadpoles. [1] [2]
  • The long posterior breathing siphon comprises two half-tubes that fit together to conduct atmospheric air from above the water surface to the respiratory system; it is not a stinger. [1] [2]
  • The raptorial front legs fold over prey and grip it; the sharp proboscis draws up internal fluids. The remaining two pairs of legs crawl along the bottom. [1]
  • The adult has two pairs of wings, protective front wings and membranous hindwings; the latter permit flight between watery habitats. [1]
  • Eggs are laid in vegetation just below the water surface; wingless adultlike nymphs develop through moults to adulthood. No pupal stage is depicted. [1]
  • The species occurs in Britain; in Norway it is recorded in the southeast and along the coast north to Bergen. [1] [2] [4]
  • The body is approximately 20 mm long excluding the breathing tube. It lives near bank vegetation over mud or clay. [4]
  • In the generalized insect respiratory system, spiracles admit air to branching tracheal tubes and finer tracheoles; oxygen diffuses to tissues and carbon dioxide diffuses out. This is a simplified functional schematic, not a species-specific dissection or measured arrangement. [3]
  • This plate combines the source-supported water-scorpion siphon with an explicitly generalized internal insect tracheal network; no exact species-specific spiracle positions or other unsupported internal organs are claimed. [1] [3]
