Bell’s Palsy — The Interrupted Facial Signal

An anatomical and clinical guide to facial nerve dysfunction, symptoms, qualified mechanism, diagnostic evaluation, eye protection, early treatment, recovery uncertainty and emergency stroke recognition.

Research behind the infographic

  • Bell's palsy usually causes weakness or paralysis on one side of the face. [1] [2]
  • The facial nerve (cranial nerve VII) connects the brain to muscles controlling facial expression; it also affects taste and ear sensation. [1]
  • The exact cause is unknown; inflammation and swelling of the facial nerve may be involved. [1]
  • Symptoms can include a drooping eyebrow or mouth, difficulty closing the eyelid, dry or watering eye, drooling and altered taste. [1] [2]
  • Symptoms develop suddenly over hours to a few days and may involve upper and lower facial muscles. [1] [2]
  • Clinicians assess facial movement and symptom onset and rule out other causes; no single laboratory test confirms Bell's palsy. [1]
  • Prompt medical assessment matters: prescribed steroids are most effective when started within 72 hours, where suitable; antivirals are sometimes added. [1] [2]
  • If the eyelid cannot close, clinician-guided lubricating drops or ointment and nighttime eyelid protection help prevent drying and eye injury. [1] [2]
  • Many people recover within weeks to six months, but recovery may take longer and some weakness can persist. [1] [2]
  • Sudden facial droop, arm weakness or speech difficulty can indicate stroke and warrant an immediate emergency call; do not self-diagnose Bell's palsy. [2]
