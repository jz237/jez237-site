Magicicada septendecim — Seventeen Years to Sing

Species-focused scientific plate of male tymbal sound production and periodical-cicada life history, with explicitly simplified internal sound anatomy.

Research behind the infographic

  • Magicicada septendecim is Linnaeus’ 17-year cicada, a true bug in family Cicadidae. [1] [3] [4]
  • Adults have black bodies, red to reddish-orange eyes, transparent wings with orange veins, and six legs; M. septendecim has broad orange bands on the abdominal underside and orange on the thorax behind each eye. [1] [3] [4]
  • Adult periodical cicadas are about one and one-half inches long; a small approximately life-size silhouette and 1 cm ruler give approximate scale rather than a measured specimen. [4] [1]
  • Periodical cicadas occur in eastern North America; the 17-year species are generally northern, with overlap with southern 13-year species. Adults use living deciduous woody vegetation. [3] [4]
  • Male periodical cicadas produce airborne songs using paired ridged membranes called tymbals on the first abdominal segment, driven by large muscles. [2] [3]
  • The male abdomen is hollow and may act as a resonating chamber; the internal sound-organ illustration is a simplified male periodical-cicada schematic, not a measured species dissection. [2]
  • Both sexes hear using membranous tympana on the abdominal underside; tympana are hearing organs, distinct from sound-producing tymbals. [2]
  • The mechanism sequence is muscle drives tymbal, membrane vibrates to produce airborne sound, and the hollow abdomen may resonate; arrows illustrate cause, not a measured oscillation frequency or displacement. [2]
  • Male calling songs attract males and females to choruses, often in high sunlit branches; female Magicicada respond to male calls with timed wing flicks. [2]
  • Nymphs live underground feeding on root fluids; M. septendecim normally has a 17-year life cycle, with occasional early or late stragglers. [1] [3] [4]
  • Mature nymphs emerge, climb vegetation and molt into pale adults that subsequently darken; there is no pupal stage. [3] [4]
  • Females insert eggs into living twigs with an ovipositor; hatchlings drop and burrow into soil to begin root feeding. [3] [4]
  • Periodical cicadas feed on plant fluids and are eaten by birds and other animals; mass emergence can satiate predators, but does not guarantee survival of each cicada. [3] [4]
