Desert Soil Crust — A Living Skin Between the Grains

A detailed dryland-ecology plate explaining biocrust community members, cyanobacterial soil binding, surface development, erosion resistance, context-dependent hydrology, physical disturbance, and variable recovery succession.

Research behind the infographic

  • Biological soil crust is a living surface community that can include cyanobacteria, lichens, mosses, green algae, microfungi, and other bacteria. [1]
  • In the biological soil crusts described for southeast Utah, cyanobacteria are dormant while dry and move through soil when wet, leaving sticky fibers that bind soil particles. [1]
  • Binding soil particles into a continuous crust helps resist erosion by wind and rain. [1] [2]
  • Young crusts in the southeast Utah example can appear flat and brown, while more mature crusts may be bumpy, dark, and include visible lichens or mosses. [1]
  • Foot traffic, bicycles, and vehicle tracks can break biological soil crust; once fragmented, pieces can be scattered by wind or rain. [1]
  • On sandy Colorado Plateau soils in one study, intact well-developed dark biocrusts had lower runoff, low sediment loss, and the highest aggregate stability, while disturbance altered these hydrologic responses. [2]
  • Biocrust hydrology is context-dependent: developmental stage, soil structure, and disturbance type can change runoff, infiltration, and sediment-loss responses. [2]
  • Natural recovery after disturbance depends on climate, soil conditions, disturbance severity, and timing, and is often dynamic rather than linear. [3]
  • A common recovery sequence begins with cyanobacteria and algae, followed later by lichens and bryophytes, but local conditions can alter that sequence. [3]
  • As biocrusts mature, they can trap fine soil particles and soil nutrient and organic-matter contents can increase. [3]
