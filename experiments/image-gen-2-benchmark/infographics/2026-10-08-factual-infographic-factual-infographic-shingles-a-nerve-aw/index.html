Shingles — A Nerve Awakens

Source-backed anatomy and clinical education on shingles reactivation, one-sided skin manifestations, PHN, urgent eye warning signs, care, transmission and vaccination.

Research behind the infographic

  • Shingles (herpes zoster) is caused by reactivation of varicella-zoster virus, the same virus that causes chickenpox. [1] [2]
  • After recovery from chickenpox, virus remains inactive in some nerve cells and may reactivate later; why reactivation occurs in some people is not fully understood. [2]
  • Reactivated virus travels in a specific nerve, commonly producing a band of rash on one side of the trunk; the face can also be affected. [2]
  • Risk increases with older age and weakened immunity, including certain diseases and immune-suppressing medicines. [1] [2]
  • Burning or tingling pain, itching or numbness may precede a red rash; fluid-filled blisters then dry and crust, followed by clearing scabs. Timing varies. [2]
  • Shingles may be painful or itchy; light touch can provoke intense pain in some people. [1] [2]
  • Postherpetic neuralgia is pain that persists in the affected area after the rash has gone; older people have a greater chance of developing it. [2]
  • Blisters on the face require prompt medical attention; blisters near or in the eye can cause lasting eye damage and blindness. [2]
  • If shingles is suspected, contact a clinician as soon as possible. Early antiviral treatment can shorten illness and reduce severity; pain relief may help. [1] [2]
  • Cool washcloths, loose clothing, keeping the area clean and avoiding scratching can help with comfort and skin care. [2]
  • A person cannot catch shingles from another person with shingles. VZV can spread from shingles blisters to susceptible people and cause chickenpox. [1] [2]
  • VZV spread can occur by direct contact with blister fluid or breathing virus particles from blisters. Virus is not spread before blisters appear or after the rash scabs over. [1]
  • Cover the rash, avoid touching or scratching it, and wash hands. Avoid contact with susceptible pregnant people, premature or low-birth-weight infants and immunocompromised people until the rash scabs over. [1]
  • CDC recommends two doses of recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) for adults age 50 and older and adults age 19 and older with weakened immune systems due to disease or therapy, to prevent shingles and related complications. [1]
