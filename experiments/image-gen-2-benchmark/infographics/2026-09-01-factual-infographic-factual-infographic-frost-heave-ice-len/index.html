Frost Heave — Ice Lenses That Lift the Ground

A source-verified direct-canvas museum infographic showing how unfrozen liquid water migrates toward a freezing zone, feeds horizontal segregated ice lenses, and can lift and crack pavement, with soil, freeze-thaw, impact, and monitoring context.

Research behind the infographic

  • Frost heave is driven chiefly by liquid-water migration toward a freezing zone and the formation and thickening of segregated ice lenses, not merely by pore water freezing in place. [1] [2]
  • Frozen soil can contain both frozen and unfrozen water, with the proportions depending on temperature depression, soil specific surface area, and salt content. [1] [2]
  • Unfrozen liquid water can migrate vertically during freezing and feed the growth of horizontal layers of solid ice called ice lenses. [1] [2]
  • Ice lenses can separate soil above from soil below and produce large vertical ground displacements called frost heave. [1]
  • Three conditions govern damaging frost action: freezing conditions, an available water supply, and frost-susceptible soil; not every wet or frozen soil heaves equally. [1]
  • Soil type and mineralogy, groundwater and flow properties, moisture content, temperature, salinity, and material properties influence freezing behavior and frost-heave severity. [1] [2]
  • Fine-grained frost-susceptible soils can support more damaging frost action than many coarser soils, but susceptibility depends on the complete soil-water-freezing system rather than texture alone. [1]
  • Frost heave can lift and damage pavement, producing roughness, displacement, and cracking above the growing ice-lens zone. [1]
  • Freeze-thaw state in pavement layers can be investigated using subsurface temperature, moisture measurements such as time-domain reflectometry, and coupled heat-moisture models. [1]
  • During thaw, ice lenses melt and previously heaved ground may lose support or settle unevenly; the exact outcome varies with soil, drainage, loading, and freeze-thaw history. [1]
