Dactylotum bicolor — The Painted Grasshopper

Painted grasshopper warning coloration, reduced wings, diet and underground seasonal cycle, with explicitly generalized digestive anatomy.

Research behind the infographic

  • The pictured or painted grasshopper is Dactylotum bicolor. [1] [2]
  • Its red and yellow warning coloration signals unpalatability to potential predators (aposematism); this does not establish a toxin or universal predator avoidance. [2]
  • Adults have a vertical face, rounded vertex, slender antennae, a punctured cylindrical pronotum with three transverse incisions, and very small tegmina and wings. [1]
  • Black and yellow bands, red-orange midline and face/thorax markings, black/red-orange hind femora and yellow-green hind tibiae characterize the adult described for Colorado. Small black tegmina have fine yellow veins. [1]
  • This flightless grasshopper occurs in shortgrass prairie and sparse low vegetation along the western Great Plains, from Alberta to northern Mexico; coloration varies geographically. [2]
  • It feeds on herbaceous flowering plants (forbs), not grasses; reported food plants include scarlet globemallow and prairie clover. [1] [2]
  • It produces one generation a year: females lay eggs in soft soil or sand in fall, eggs overwinter underground, and hatch in late spring to early summer. [2]
  • Eggs are brownish-yellow in underground pods, laid in disorderly columns; adult length is about 25 mm for males and 32 mm for females in the cited account. [1]
  • The supporting internal view is explicitly a simplified generalized insect digestive plan, not a dissection of Dactylotum: a mouth-to-anus alimentary canal with foregut, midgut and hindgut. [3]
  • In the generalized insect gut, the crop stores food, the midgut is the main site of enzymatic digestion and nutrient absorption, and the hindgut regulates water and salt recovery from waste. [3]
