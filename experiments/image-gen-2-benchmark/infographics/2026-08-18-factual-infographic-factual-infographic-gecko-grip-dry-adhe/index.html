Gecko Grip — Dry Adhesion Without Glue

A museum-quality direct-canvas guide to how Tokay gecko toe pads use branching setae, spatula contact, van der Waals attraction, directional loading, and peeling release to create reversible dry adhesion.

Research behind the infographic

  • Tokay gecko toe pads carry microscopic hairs called setae, and each seta branches into much smaller terminal pads called spatulae. [1] [2]
  • The hierarchical branching from toe pad to setae to spatulae creates extensive close contact with a surface. [1] [2]
  • Experiments support van der Waals forces as the primary mechanism of dry adhesion in Tokay gecko setae. [1]
  • Gecko dry adhesion does not require secreted glue or suction. [1] [2]
  • Tokay gecko setae adhered similarly to strongly hydrophobic and strongly hydrophilic surfaces when both surfaces were polarizable, arguing against capillary adhesion as necessary. [1]
  • Simply touching a seta to a surface is insufficient for strong attachment; loading it toward the surface and slightly along the surface engages adhesion. [2]
  • The directional geometry of setae and spatulae makes attachment dependent on loading direction. [1] [2]
  • Geckos detach rapidly by changing angle and peeling their toes away, which progressively reduces contact rather than breaking every contact at once. [2]
  • The adhesive system works without leaving a glue residue on the contacted surface. [2]
  • Setal-tip size and shape, rather than high surface polarity, are central to the measured adhesion mechanism. [1]
  • Subdividing an adhesive surface into many small contacts increases contact density and is a design principle for gecko-inspired dry adhesives. [1]
  • Natural surfaces can be more challenging than clean laboratory glass; waxy leaf coatings may hinder close intermolecular contact. [2]
  • The Tokay gecko, Gekko gecko, is native to Southeast Asia. [2]
