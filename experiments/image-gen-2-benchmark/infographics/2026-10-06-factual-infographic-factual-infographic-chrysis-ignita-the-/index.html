Chrysis ignita — The Armored Curl

Species-focused anatomy and family-level defensive curling, with eight supporting panels, prominent biological close-ups and explicitly qualified host/development information.

Research behind the infographic

  • Chrysis ignita is a cuckoo wasp; its close lookalikes complicate identification and older host records. [1]
  • Chrysis ignita body length is 5–10 mm; all illustrated macro views are enlarged. [1]
  • The head and mesosoma are shiny blue or violet, with green reflections on the pronotum and mesoscutellum; tergites are golden red and sternites green or blue. [1]
  • The mesoscutum has punctures the same colour as the interstices; T2 and T3 have coarse regular punctation and sharply produced apical teeth. [1]
  • The frontal carina of Chrysis ignita is shallowly M-shaped or arcuate, not four tooth-like tubercles. [1]
  • The species occurs from western Europe to central Asia and China; distribution records can be confused by unrevised identifications. [1]
  • Habitats include gardens, parks and forest margins; adults occur on old wooden or stone walls, dead tree trunks, poles and log piles. [1]
  • Cuckoo wasps have a ventrally hollowed abdomen; most Chrysidinae can curl into a defensive ball (conglobulation). Females disturbed or attacked fold into a compact ball, shielding vulnerable body parts. [2]
  • Chrysidid exoskeletons are heavily sculptured and very tough, providing armor against host stings; this is protection rather than invulnerability. [3]
  • At family level, cuckoo-wasp immature stages include egg, larva, cocoon/pupa and adult; many develop in solitary-wasp or bee nests, as parasitoids or consumers of host provisions. Exact feeding habits differ among species. [2]
  • Ancistrocerus parietum is only a probable host of Chrysis ignita; numerous earlier host records are unreliable because of inconsistent taxonomy. [1]
  • Adult insects depicted have six legs, antennae, head, mesosoma and metasoma; the frontal ridge, sculptured body and wings are shown as anatomical views rather than proof of identification from colour alone. [1] [2]
