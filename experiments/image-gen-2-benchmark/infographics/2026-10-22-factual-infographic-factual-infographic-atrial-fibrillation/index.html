Atrial Fibrillation — An Irregular Rhythm

Artwork-led anatomical atlas of atrial fibrillation, reduced coordinated atrial contraction, possible clot-related stroke, symptoms, assessment and individualized care.

Research behind the infographic

  • Atrial fibrillation (AFib) is an irregular heart rhythm; the two upper chambers are the atria, and the two lower chambers are the ventricles. [1] [2]
  • In AFib atrial beating is irregular and atria and ventricles do not work together as they should, reducing efficient blood flow from atria to ventricles. [1] [2]
  • Blood may pool and form clots in the heart; a clot can travel to and block an artery supplying the brain, causing ischemic stroke. This is a possible complication, not inevitable. [1] [2] [6]
  • The left atrial appendage is a small sac in the wall of the left atrium where clots can form. [3]
  • AFib may cause fluttering or pounding palpitations, fatigue, shortness of breath, and lightheadedness, or no symptoms. [1] [2]
  • Risk increases with age; high blood pressure, sleep apnea and excess alcohol are among risk factors, not a single universal cause. [5]
  • Clinician assessment and an electrocardiogram (ECG/EKG) diagnose AFib; longer monitoring may detect intermittent episodes. [4]
  • Management can include heart-rate control, rhythm control with medicines, electrical cardioversion or catheter ablation in selected people, and management of underlying risk factors. Treatment is individualized. [3] [6]
  • Anticoagulants may lower stroke risk when indicated after clinical assessment, but carry bleeding risk; rhythm treatment does not mean stroke-risk assessment can be ignored. [3] [6]
  • Treating high blood pressure and sleep apnea and discussing alcohol use with a clinician are relevant elements of AFib care. [3] [6]
  • Call 911 immediately for sudden weakness or trouble speaking, or chest pressure lasting minutes or returning, which can indicate stroke or heart attack. Do not dismiss these as routine AFib symptoms. [6]
