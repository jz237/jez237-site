Salt Marsh — Roots, Mud, and Carbon

An illustrated cross-section of salt-marsh carbon pathways and surface elevation gain through flood-tide sediment deposition and root accumulation.

Research behind the infographic

  • Salt marsh vegetation takes up atmospheric carbon dioxide through photosynthesis and uses carbon to grow leaves, stems, and roots. [1] [3]
  • Flood tides carry sediment into a marsh, where some is trapped and added to the marsh surface. [3]
  • Root growth and accumulated root material add belowground material that can raise marsh surface elevation. [3]
  • Dead leaves and roots can become incorporated and buried in salt-marsh soil. [1] [3]
  • Water-saturated wetland soil is largely oxygen-poor because oxygen diffuses slowly through water. [1]
  • Organic material decomposes more slowly in oxygen-poor soil, allowing some carbon to remain stored underground for long periods. [1]
  • Some captured carbon returns to air through plant or microbial respiration; not all carbon is permanently retained. [1]
  • Salt-marsh sediment carbon may come both from marsh plants and from organic material transported in from elsewhere; a USGS comparison found source mixtures differed between a restoring and reference marsh. [2]
  • Marsh persistence amid rising sea level depends on surface elevation gain, including root-material accumulation and flood-tide sediment deposition; ability to keep pace varies with conditions. [3]
