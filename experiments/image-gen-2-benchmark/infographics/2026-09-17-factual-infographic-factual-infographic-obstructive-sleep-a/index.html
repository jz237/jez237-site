Obstructive Sleep Apnea — The Airway Under Pressure

A source-verified medical anatomy infographic explaining upper-airway obstruction, continued respiratory effort, sleep and daytime clues, risk context, objective sleep testing, CPAP mechanics, individualized treatment categories, and safety context.

Research behind the infographic

  • Obstructive sleep apnea occurs when the upper airway becomes repeatedly narrowed or blocked during sleep, reducing or stopping airflow. [1] [2]
  • The tongue and soft palate can move backward during sleep and contribute to upper-airway obstruction. [2]
  • Obstructive apnea involves an airway blockage, whereas central sleep apnea results from missing brain signals needed to breathe. [1] [2]
  • Common sleep-time clues include breathing that starts and stops, frequent loud snoring, and gasping for air. [3]
  • Daytime sleepiness and tiredness can impair learning, focus, and reaction. [3]
  • Risk factors and associations include older age, obesity, a large tongue or tonsils, family history, some endocrine disorders, smoking, and alcohol use; these factors do not by themselves diagnose OSA. [2]
  • A clinical evaluation considers symptoms, risk factors, and family history, and a sleep study can identify the apnea type and severity. [4]
  • Positive-airway-pressure treatment should follow an OSA diagnosis established with objective sleep apnea testing. [6]
  • CPAP provides continuous air pressure through the nose and/or mouth to help keep the airway open during sleep. [5]
  • Treatment options can include individualized lifestyle changes, PAP, custom oral appliances, and selected surgical procedures; the appropriate choice depends on the person and clinical assessment. [5] [6]
  • PAP therapy requires education, troubleshooting, monitoring, and follow-up rather than a one-size-fits-all setup. [6]
  • Untreated sleep apnea can impair concentration, decision-making, memory, and behavior and is associated with increased risk of stroke, heart attack, and other serious problems. [1]
  • People with possible symptoms should discuss them with a healthcare provider; a sleep study may be needed, especially when daytime sleepiness affects attention or reaction. [3] [4]
