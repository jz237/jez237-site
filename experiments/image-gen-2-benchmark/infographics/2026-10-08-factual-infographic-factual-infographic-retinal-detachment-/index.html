Retinal Detachment — The Lifting Light Layer

Anatomical cutaway and eight source-backed panels explain detachment mechanisms, warning signs, risk factors, diagnosis, repair and prevention limits.

Research behind the infographic

  • The retina is light-sensitive tissue lining the back of the eye; it converts entering light into electrical signals sent to the brain through the optic nerve. [1] [2]
  • Retinal detachment separates the retina from its normal position at the back wall and underlying blood supply, impairing function and risking permanent vision loss. [1] [2]
  • In the most common, tear-related (rhegmatogenous) type, fluid passes through a retinal hole or tear and collects beneath the retina, lifting it away. [2]
  • Aging-related separation of vitreous gel from the retina can cause a retinal tear; thinning peripheral retina and trauma can also lead to tears. [2]
  • Tractional detachment occurs when scar tissue pulls the retina away; diabetes or other conditions can cause it. [2]
  • Exudative detachment occurs when leaking vessels allow fluid to accumulate beneath the retina; inflammation or abnormal vessels can be involved. [2]
  • Warning signs include a sudden increase in floaters, flashes of light, and a dark curtain or shadow in the field of vision; some small detachments cause no symptoms. [1] [2]
  • Retinal detachment is a medical emergency; symptoms require an eye doctor or emergency room right away, and prompt treatment can help protect vision. [1]
  • Risk factors include extreme nearsightedness, advancing age, previous eye surgery, serious eye injury, previous detachment, and family history; risk factors do not mean detachment is inevitable. [1] [2]
  • Diagnosis uses a dilated eye exam to inspect the retina, including its periphery; OCT or ultrasound may help when more information is needed. [1] [2]
  • Laser or freezing treatment can seal retinal tears; a larger detachment may require surgery to put the retina back into place. [1] [2]
  • A scleral buckle is a silicone band outside the eye wall that pushes it closer to a retinal tear; freezing treatment seals the tear. [2]
  • Vitrectomy removes vitreous gel and drains fluid from beneath the retina; laser or freezing seals tears and a gas bubble can hold the retina while it heals. [2]
  • Pneumatic retinopexy uses a gas bubble and specific head posture to position the bubble over a tear, with laser or freezing to seal it. [2]
  • A retina specialist selects treatment based on the detachment; more than one procedure may be needed and reattachment does not guarantee complete visual recovery. [2]
  • The macula is the center of the retina; involvement of this central area affects visual prognosis. [2]
  • Many aging-related detachments cannot be prevented; protective eyewear during risky activities reduces injury risk and regular dilated eye exams can find small tears or detachments early. [1]
