Peptic Ulcers — A Break in the Digestive Lining

An artistic cutaway atlas of stomach and duodenal ulcers, H. pylori and NSAID causes, diagnostic evaluation, treatment and emergency warning signs.

Research behind the infographic

  • A peptic ulcer is a sore in the lining of the stomach or duodenum; the two locations are alternatives, not necessarily simultaneous. [1] [3]
  • Helicobacter pylori infection and NSAID medicines are the most common causes of peptic ulcers. [1] [2] [3]
  • NSAIDs such as ibuprofen can make the stomach lining more prone to damage and ulcers; this is a risk, not a universal outcome. [2]
  • Symptoms can include upper abdominal burning or discomfort, feeling full early, nausea and bloating; some people have no symptoms. [1] [2] [3]
  • Food can relieve pain briefly in some people and worsen it in others; meal timing cannot diagnose an ulcer. [2]
  • Evaluation may use H. pylori breath or stool tests and sometimes upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. [1] [3]
  • Treatment targets the cause, including antibiotics for H. pylori infection and acid-reducing medicines such as proton pump inhibitors. [3]
  • A clinician may review or change NSAID medicines; an infographic should not direct independent changes to prescribed medicines. [1] [3]
  • Follow-up tests may be needed to confirm treatment has worked. [3]
  • Ulcers may cause bleeding or perforation; these are possible complications, not present in every ulcer. [1] [3]
  • Vomiting blood or coffee-ground material, black tarry stool, sudden severe persistent abdominal pain or fainting needs urgent or emergency assessment. [2] [3]
  • Diet is not considered an important primary cause or treatment for peptic ulcers; foods may worsen symptoms for some people and do not replace treating H. pylori or medication causes. [1] [3]
  • This infographic provides general education, not a diagnosis or an individualized treatment plan. [1] [3]
