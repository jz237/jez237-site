Atmospheric Rivers — Water in Motion

A source-verified museum-quality direct-canvas infographic showing how a long, narrow corridor of wind-driven atmospheric water vapor crosses an ocean, rises over coastal mountains, and produces rain or snow, with both water-supply benefits and flood or slope hazards.

Research behind the infographic

  • Atmospheric rivers are relatively long, narrow regions of enhanced water-vapor transport in the atmosphere. [1] [2]
  • Atmospheric rivers transport much of the water vapor moving outside the tropics and can carry moisture from lower latitudes toward higher latitudes. [1] [2]
  • Water vapor within an atmospheric river is transported by winds, so both moisture content and wind contribute to vapor transport. [1] [2]
  • Atmospheric-river water vapor is concentrated primarily in the lower atmosphere rather than forming a liquid river in the sky. [2]
  • When moist air reaches land and is forced upward over mountains, cooling and condensation can release the transported water as rain or snow. [1] [2]
  • Atmospheric rivers vary greatly in size, strength, shape, and impacts. [1] [2]
  • Weaker atmospheric rivers can provide beneficial rain and snowpack that support water supplies. [1] [2]
  • Strong or slow-moving atmospheric rivers can produce extreme precipitation, flooding, landslides, and debris-flow hazards. [1] [2]
  • Satellite, radar, aircraft, and weather-model observations are used to study and forecast atmospheric rivers. [1]
  • Integrated vapor transport is a useful way to represent the combined movement of atmospheric water vapor by the wind. [1] [2]
  • Atmospheric rivers are not all hazardous; their effects depend on intensity, duration, landfall location, terrain, and watershed vulnerability. [1] [2]
