Regimbartia attenuata — The Beetle That Escapes Through a Frog

A source-verified direct-canvas museum infographic showing the tiny aquatic beetle, its macro anatomy, the observed live passage through a frog, the carefully qualified gut-stimulation hypothesis, paddy-field context, scale, and known larval instars.

Research behind the infographic

  • Regimbartia attenuata (Fabricius, 1801) is an aquatic water scavenger beetle in the family Hydrophilidae and tribe Berosini. [1] [2]
  • Adults used in the laboratory study measured 3.8–5.0 mm long. [1]
  • Regimbartia attenuata and the frog Pelophylax nigromaculatus frequently occur together in Japanese paddy fields, where the frog can attack aquatic insects. [1]
  • In laboratory feeding trials, frogs readily swallowed adult Regimbartia attenuata, yet 93.3% of the swallowed beetles were excreted alive and active within six hours; observed passage times were 0.1–3.5 hours. [1]
  • The study's observations indicate that swallowed beetles actively moved through the esophagus, stomach, small intestine, and large intestine toward the frog's cloacal aperture rather than passing only as inert remains. [1]
  • Because the frog's sphincter normally keeps the vent closed, the authors cautiously proposed that the beetle may stimulate the frog's gut to promote excretion; this is an inference rather than a directly demonstrated mechanism. [1]
  • Undigested remains of other prey took more than 24 hours to be excreted in the comparison described by the study. [1]
  • All three larval instars of Regimbartia attenuata have been described from reared and field-collected Japanese material, supporting a distinct immature-stage context without implying that larvae perform the adult gut-escape behavior. [2]
