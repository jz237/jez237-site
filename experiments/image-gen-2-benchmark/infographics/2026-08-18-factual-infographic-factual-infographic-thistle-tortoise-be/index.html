Thistle Tortoise Beetle — The Moving Fecal Shield

A direct-canvas insect-first infographic explaining how Cassida rubiginosa larvae assemble feces and cast skins on a forked caudal process and move the shield to block ant bites.

Research behind the infographic

  • Cassida rubiginosa is the thistle tortoise beetle, a real leaf beetle in family Chrysomelidae and subfamily Cassidinae. [2]
  • Larvae are green, have marginal spines, and carry shield material on a forked caudal process held over the back. [1] [2]
  • The carried shield packet contains feces and retained cast skins (exuviae). [1] [2] [3]
  • The larva can maneuver the shield as a physical defense, and experiments found it effective at blocking ant bites. [1]
  • Cassidine shield construction uses caudal processes and deliberate manipulation of fecal and exuvial material; shield architecture and retention vary among tortoise beetle lineages. [3]
  • Cassida rubiginosa adults and larvae feed on thistle foliage; Canada thistle is a primary North American host. [2]
  • Young larvae window-feed on leaf undersides, while older larvae make irregular feeding holes. [2]
  • The life cycle proceeds through egg, five larval instars, pupa, and adult, with one generation per year in the described North American context. [2]
  • Adults are oval and green with a hard protective covering and black underside; larvae reach roughly 6 mm and adults up to roughly 7.5 mm in the cited North American profile. [2]
  • The species is native to Europe and has been used as a biological-control herbivore of Canada thistle in North America and New Zealand. [2]
