Franciscan Blister Beetle — The Living Bee Decoy

A direct-canvas macro-entomology infographic shows how tiny Franciscan blister beetle triungulins cooperate as a living female-bee decoy, transfer from male to female bee, and reach the host nest.

Research behind the infographic

  • Meloe franciscanus is a blister beetle whose first-instar larvae are called triungulins. [1]
  • The triungulins are approximately two millimeters long, six-legged insect larvae. [1]
  • In the southwestern United States study system, Meloe franciscanus and its solitary-bee host Habropoda pallida inhabit desert sand dunes where Astragalus plants form scattered resource patches. [1]
  • Newly emerged triungulins climb vegetation and cooperate in a tight dark aggregation positioned like a perched female host bee. [1] [2]
  • The larval aggregation mimics visual features of a female bee, including approximate size, color, and perching location. [1] [2]
  • The aggregation also produces a volatile chemical cue that mimics components of the female host bee's sex pheromone and attracts male bees. [1]
  • Visual mimicry alone was not sufficient in field tests; chemical cues were the primary means of attracting male bees. [1]
  • When a male Habropoda pallida contacts the aggregation in an attempted mating interaction, the triungulins attach to his body as a group. [1] [2]
  • Triungulins can transfer from the male bee to a female bee during mating or attempted mating contact. [1] [2]
  • The female bee carries the triungulins to her nest, where they dismount and develop as nest parasites. [1]
  • Inside the host nest, the beetle larvae consume pollen and nectar provisions and the host bee egg before completing development. [1]
  • The complete transport sequence is vegetation aggregation to male bee to female bee to host nest. [1] [2]
