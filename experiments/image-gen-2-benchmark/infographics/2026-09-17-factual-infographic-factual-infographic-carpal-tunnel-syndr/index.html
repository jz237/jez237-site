Carpal Tunnel Syndrome — Pressure on the Median Nerve

A source-verified medical anatomy infographic explaining the confined carpal tunnel, median-nerve compression, sensory territory, symptoms, associated factors, evaluation, management pathways, and timely assessment for progressive nerve dysfunction.

Research behind the infographic

  • The carpal tunnel is a narrow, rigid wrist passage bounded by carpal bones and the transverse carpal ligament. [1] [2]
  • The median nerve and finger-flexor tendons pass through the carpal tunnel. [1] [2]
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome occurs when pressure within this confined passage squeezes the median nerve, producing numbness, tingling, pain, or weakness. [1] [2]
  • Median-nerve sensation in the hand includes the thumb, index finger, middle finger, and thumb-side half of the ring finger; the nerve also controls muscles around the base of the thumb. [2]
  • Symptoms commonly begin gradually and may be worse at night or during prolonged activities with the wrist bent; shaking or moving the hand may temporarily ease symptoms. [1] [2]
  • Hand clumsiness, dropping objects, persistent numbness, and weakness or wasting at the thumb base can occur as nerve dysfunction becomes more severe or prolonged. [1] [2]
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome usually reflects a combination of factors; associated factors include wrist anatomy or injury, pregnancy-related swelling, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, thyroid disease, repeated hand motions, and prolonged extreme wrist flexion or extension. [1] [2]
  • Long-term typing or computer use alone has not been established as a definite cause of carpal tunnel syndrome. [1]
  • Clinical evaluation begins with symptom and medical history plus examination of sensation and thumb-base strength; provocative wrist maneuvers may reproduce tingling but do not replace the full evaluation. [1] [2]
  • When needed, nerve-conduction studies and electromyography can assess nerve and muscle function, while ultrasound can show the median nerve and X-rays can help assess other wrist causes. [1] [2]
  • A neutral-position night wrist splint and modification of aggravating activities are common nonsurgical management options; clinician-guided nerve-gliding exercises or corticosteroid injection may be considered for selected patients. [1] [2]
  • Corticosteroid injection may provide temporary symptom relief but is not guaranteed to provide long-term benefit. [2]
  • Carpal tunnel release surgery divides the transverse carpal ligament to enlarge the tunnel and reduce pressure on the median nerve; it may be considered when symptoms are severe, testing shows significant nerve changes, or nonsurgical care is insufficient. [1] [2]
  • Persistent numbness, worsening weakness, or visible thumb-base muscle wasting warrants timely clinical assessment because prolonged compression can cause lasting nerve dysfunction. [1] [2]
