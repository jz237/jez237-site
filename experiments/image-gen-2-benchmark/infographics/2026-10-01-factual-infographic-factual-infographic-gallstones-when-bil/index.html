Gallstones — When Bile Flow Is Blocked

Detailed biliary anatomy and eight panels explaining stone formation, blockage, clinical features, risk, assessment, management and warning signs.

Research behind the infographic

  • The pear-shaped gallbladder lies below the liver in the upper right abdomen and stores bile produced by the liver. [1]
  • Bile passes through hepatic, cystic and common bile ducts into the duodenum; the pancreas also delivers digestive juices to the duodenum. [1]
  • Eating signals the gallbladder to release stored bile into the duodenum to mix with food. [1]
  • Gallstones are hard pebble-like material formed in the gallbladder; cholesterol stones are typically yellow-green and pigment stones are dark and made of bilirubin. [1]
  • Stones may form when bile has too much cholesterol or bilirubin, insufficient bile salts, or the gallbladder empties incompletely or too infrequently; the reasons for bile changes are not fully understood. [2]
  • Most gallstones are silent and do not cause symptoms; asymptomatic gallbladder stones usually need no treatment. [1] [2] [3] [4]
  • A stone obstructing a bile duct can cause bile buildup and biliary colic; attacks often stop when the stone moves and the blockage ends. [2]
  • Pain can be severe and constant, in the upper right or middle abdomen, last several hours, and accompany nausea or vomiting; attacks often follow heavy meals. [2] [4]
  • Persistent blockage can lead to gallbladder inflammation, bile-duct infection, or gallstone pancreatitis; these are possible complications, not inevitable outcomes. [1] [2] [4]
  • Risk is higher with older age, family history, obesity, rapid weight loss, and in women; risk factors do not establish an individual diagnosis. [1] [4]
  • Evaluation of suspected gallstones can include ultrasound and blood tests; similar symptoms may have other causes requiring clinical assessment. [2] [4]
  • The main treatment for symptomatic gallstones is gallbladder removal, often laparoscopic; ERCP may clear a stone in the common bile duct. Treatment depends on clinical circumstances. [3] [4]
  • After gallbladder removal, bile flows from the liver through the ducts directly into the duodenum rather than being stored in a gallbladder. [3]
  • Healthy eating with fiber, regular physical activity, maintaining a healthy weight and avoiding rapid weight loss can help reduce gallstone risk; they do not guarantee prevention or dissolve existing stones. [1] [3] [4]
  • Seek urgent medical care for sudden severe abdominal pain, pain lasting several hours, fever or chills, jaundice, or pain spreading to the back with vomiting; dark urine and pale stools may accompany complications. [2] [4]
