Tennis Elbow — The Outer Tendon Attachment

Anatomical atlas of lateral epicondylitis, load-related symptoms, clinical assessment and conservative management with safety context.

Research behind the infographic

  • The elbow contains the humerus, radius and ulna; the lateral epicondyle is the outer bony prominence of the humerus. [1]
  • Tendons connect muscle to bone; the ECRB wrist extensor tendon attaches at the lateral epicondyle and is commonly involved in tennis elbow. [1] [2]
  • Repeated vigorous forearm use can damage the tendon attachment, with degeneration and sometimes microscopic tears; this is not necessarily a complete tendon rupture. [1]
  • Gripping, repetitive wrist extension or twisting in work, hobbies and racquet sports can contribute; tennis is not required and sometimes no repetitive injury is identified. [1] [2]
  • Symptoms include outside-elbow pain and tenderness, weak grip, and pain worsened by gripping or wrist movement; symptoms often develop gradually. [1] [2]
  • Assessment uses symptom history and examination; tests or imaging may be used to exclude other causes, not necessarily required for everyone. [1]
  • Reduce activities that worsen symptoms; physiotherapy with wrist and forearm stretching and strengthening can help; a forearm strap or brace may ease symptoms. [1] [2]
  • Surgery is considered for persistent symptoms after prolonged nonsurgical treatment, not an automatic first step. [1] [2]
  • Seek medical review for persistent pain despite self-care. [2]
  • A hot swollen elbow with fever or chills, or severe pain with difficulty moving, warrants urgent assessment; injury with deformity or snapping also needs urgent assessment. [3]
  • Sudden arm pain with chest pressure, heaviness or squeezing requires emergency help; do not assume tennis elbow. [3]
