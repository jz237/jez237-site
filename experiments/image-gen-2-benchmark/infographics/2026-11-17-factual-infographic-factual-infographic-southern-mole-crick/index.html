Southern Mole Cricket — The Underground Calling Horn

Flowing species atlas of digging forelegs, calling burrow, life stages and a clearly generalized insect nervous-system schematic.

Research behind the infographic

  • Southern mole cricket is Neoscapteriscus borellii, formerly Scapteriscus borellii. [1] [2] [5]
  • Adults are brown, with enlarged digging forelegs, forewings longer than the pronotum and hindwings extending beyond the abdomen; body length is 25–32 mm. [1] [5]
  • Neoscapteriscus has two blade-like dactyls on each foretibia; in N. borellii they are separated at the base by at least half a dactyl basal width. [1] [2]
  • Mole crickets force loose soil aside with strokes of the enlarged forelegs and spend nearly all their lives underground. [3]
  • Southern mole cricket habitat includes moist sandy or mucky open areas such as lawns, fields and pond or stream margins. [1]
  • The species is native to South America and introduced to the southeastern United States. [1] [2]
  • Males call with a low ringing trill from a horn-shaped burrow opening; specialized calling burrows augment and project sound skyward, attracting flying conspecifics including females. [1] [2] [3]
  • A generalized Neoscapteriscus calling burrow contains an opening horn, constrictions, a bulb, a turn-around and surface/deep tunnels. [4]
  • Eggs are gray-brown, bean-shaped, laid in underground chambers; nymphs resemble smaller wingless adults. [5]
  • Southern mole crickets are largely carnivorous, though omnivorous; they mostly eat other insects. [1] [5]
  • A generalized insect nervous system has a dorsal brain connected to a ventral nerve cord with segmental ganglia; thoracic ganglia innervate legs and wings. This is a generalized schematic, not a measured species dissection. [6]
  • One generation per year is typical except in south Florida, where there can be two; eggs are laid in spring and large nymphs or adults overwinter. [1]
