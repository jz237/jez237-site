Aurora — From Magnetosphere to Photon

An eight-panel source-backed educational plate tracing magnetospheric energy transfer, electron precipitation, excitation, emission color, collision quenching and auroral geometry.

Research behind the infographic

  • Solar wind transfers energy into Earth’s magnetosphere; solar wind pressure stretches a magnetotail away from the Sun. [1] [2]
  • Oppositely directed solar-wind and terrestrial magnetic fields favor energy transfer through magnetic reconnection. [1] [2]
  • Auroral electrons are accelerated within the magnetosphere; nighttime auroral electrons can come from the magnetotail, rather than arriving directly as solar-wind electrons. [2]
  • Accelerated electrons follow magnetic field lines into the upper atmosphere near magnetic poles. [1] [2]
  • Collisions transfer energy from electrons to atmospheric atoms and molecules, exciting them; relaxation releases photons. [1] [2]
  • Excited atomic oxygen produces green and red light; nitrogen can produce blue and pink-purple light. [1] [2]
  • Red oxygen emission is favored higher in the thinner atmosphere; green oxygen emission and pink-purple nitrogen lower borders can occur below it; these are tendencies, not rigid altitude boundaries. [1] [2]
  • The excited oxygen state producing red light is longer lived than the green-emitting state; collisions can quench red emission in denser lower air before a photon is emitted. [2]
  • Auroral emissions form ovals around magnetic poles, not a single spot over the geographic pole. [2]
  • Quiet displays can be simple arcs; greater geomagnetic activity can yield brighter multiple arcs, rays, waves and curls, and expand the oval toward lower latitudes. [2]
  • Cameras can detect auroral colors too dim for human eyes; faint auroras may look pale to a person because night vision favors brightness over color. [1] [2]
