Titiwai — The Living Fishing Curtain

An insect-first direct-canvas infographic showing how the real New Zealand glow-worm larva uses blue-green bioluminescence, hanging silk lines, and hygroscopic adhesive droplets to lure, trap, and retrieve small prey.

Research behind the infographic

  • The New Zealand glow-worm is the real insect species Arachnocampa luminosa, a fungus-gnat larva rather than a worm. [1] [2]
  • Arachnocampa luminosa is endemic to New Zealand and occurs in still, dark, damp caves and forest habitats with suitable prey. [1] [2]
  • The larva lives suspended in a horizontal mucous tube attached to a ceiling or overhang. [1] [2]
  • The larva secretes silk through mouthpart-associated labial glands and lowers multiple vertical fishing lines from its tube. [1] [2]
  • The fishing lines carry regularly spaced adhesive mucous droplets that trap small insects. [1] [2]
  • The adhesive droplets are mostly water, are hygroscopic, and contain dissolved elements and peptide material; they differ from orb-spider glue droplets. [2]
  • A light organ near the larva's posterior end is associated with the Malpighian-tubule system and emits blue-green bioluminescence. [1] [2]
  • The larval glow functions as a prey lure, while the hanging adhesive lines perform the capture. [1] [2]
  • After prey sticks to a line, the larva can use its mouthparts to haul the line upward and reach the prey. [1] [2]
  • Fishing-line length varies with habitat and wind exposure, with longer lines favored in still, moist cave conditions and shorter lines in wind-prone forest habitat. [1] [2]
  • Larvae can reach about 30 millimeters before pupation, providing a supported scale cue for the whole-insect view. [1]
  • The life cycle proceeds through egg, luminous predatory larva, pupa, and winged adult fungus gnat; the larval stage is the principal feeding stage. [1] [2]
