Giant Salmonfly — Gills Beneath the Current

Source-backed natural-history plate of the giant salmonfly nymph’s ventral gill tufts, shredding ecology, habitat, and aquatic-to-terrestrial life cycle.

Research behind the infographic

  • The giant salmonfly is Pteronarcys californica, a stonefly in order Plecoptera and family Pteronarcyidae. [1]
  • The aquatic nymph is a large-bodied insect; the terrestrial adult is winged. Depictions are enlarged, not life-size. [1] [3]
  • Tracheal gill tufts are located ventrally on thoracic segments of the nymph, and their filaments are part of its oxygen uptake/delivery respiratory system. [2]
  • Nymphs live on the river bottom among cobbles in swift, well-oxygenated water. [1] [3]
  • Nymphs are shredders that feed on leaf and other large organic material collected among rocks or debris. [1]
  • The species occurs in western North American rivers, including the Coast, Cascade, Rocky and Sierra Nevada mountain ranges. [1] [3]
  • Nymphs spend multiple years in water and grow through repeated molts; duration varies, so no single fixed duration is shown. [1] [3]
  • Mature nymphs crawl to shore and climb out of water before the final molt into terrestrial adults. [1] [3]
  • Adults have an orange or red band behind the head, orange/red underside of the abdomen, and broad dark-veined wings. [1]
  • Females lay eggs on or above water; eggs separate and sink to the bottom to begin the next generation. [1]
  • Salmonflies are food for aquatic and terrestrial consumers, including trout and insect-eating birds; nymph feeding processes organic material. [1] [3]
  • Warming and low oxygen can interact to affect nymph physiology; responses vary with population and conditions, with no universal threshold depicted. [2] [3]
