Kidney Stones — A Crystal's Journey

A source-verified medical anatomy infographic tracing crystal formation and stone passage through the urinary tract, with symptom patterns, obstruction, laboratory and imaging evaluation, treatment pathways, individualized prevention, and urgent warning signs.

Research behind the infographic

  • Kidney stones are solid crystal build-ups formed from substances present in urine. [1] [5]
  • High urinary levels of calcium, oxalate, and phosphorus can contribute to kidney-stone formation. [1]
  • A stone can move from a kidney into a ureter and obstruct urine flow. [2] [5]
  • Kidney-stone pain may be felt in the back, side, lower abdomen, or groin and may come and go in waves. [1]
  • Blood in urine, painful or urgent urination, nausea, vomiting, fever, chills, and inability to urinate can occur with kidney stones and require healthcare assessment. [1] [5]
  • Urinalysis can detect blood, stone-forming minerals, white blood cells, or bacteria, while blood tests can identify elevated mineral levels. [2]
  • CT can show a stone's size and location and whether it blocks the urinary tract; ultrasound can help identify stones and blocked urinary flow. [2] [5]
  • Treatment is selected according to stone size, location, composition, symptoms, and whether urine flow is blocked. [3] [5]
  • Some small stones pass without a procedure, while larger, painful, or obstructing stones may require urgent treatment. [3]
  • Procedure categories include shock-wave lithotripsy, ureteroscopy, and percutaneous nephrolithotomy; a ureteral stent or nephrostomy may restore drainage when flow is blocked. [3] [5]
  • Analyzing a recovered stone and, when indicated, urine testing can help guide prevention according to stone type and individual risk. [3] [4]
  • Adequate fluid intake helps prevent many kidney stones, but the appropriate amount and dietary changes should be individualized for health conditions and stone type. [3] [4]
