Dentophobia — Waiting Beside the Dental Chair

Source-backed educational dentophobia plate distinguishing fear, sensory triggers, variable physical symptoms, care avoidance, realistic pain concerns and tailored support.

Research behind the infographic

  • Dental anxiety can occur before a visit; severe persistent dental fear can lead to avoidance. Ordinary nervousness does not itself establish a phobia. [3]
  • Dental fear can be associated with feared pain, previous difficult experiences, unfamiliar procedures, or a perceived lack of control. [1] [3]
  • The sights of dental instruments, drilling sounds and vibrations, clinical smells, and the reclined dental-chair position can trigger anxiety for some people. [3]
  • Uncertainty about what will happen can contribute to anxiety; asking questions about the procedure can help reduce fear of the unknown. [1] [3]
  • Possible anxiety responses include a noticeable heartbeat, trembling, dizziness, chest tightness, feeling sick, muscle tension and sweaty palms; symptoms vary. [2] [3]
  • Avoiding dental care can increase the risk of dental problems and make later visits more difficult, reinforcing fear; this is a possible cycle, not an inevitable outcome. [1] [3]
  • Fear can concern perceived danger; pain concerns should not be dismissed. Patients should discuss pain management and tell the dentist if pain occurs even with local anesthetic. [1] [3]
  • Tell the dental team about anxiety and previous experiences; ask what will happen and agree on a raised-hand signal to pause. [1]
  • Headphones for bothersome drilling sounds, a handheld stress ball, slow breathing and relaxing tense muscles can help manage dental anxiety. [1]
  • CBT and professionally guided exposure-based approaches can help with dental phobia; management should be tailored to the person and clinical situation. [2] [3]
  • Seek professional help when phobia symptoms affect life; a dentist and mental-health professional may work together on care. [2] [3]
