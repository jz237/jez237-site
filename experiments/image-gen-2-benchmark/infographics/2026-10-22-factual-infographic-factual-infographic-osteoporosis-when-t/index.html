Osteoporosis — When the Inner Scaffold Thins

Artwork-led anatomical atlas showing loss of cortical and trabecular strength, vertebral compression and evidence-based fracture prevention and evaluation.

Research behind the infographic

  • Osteoporosis reduces bone mass and structural strength, increasing the risk of broken bones. [1] [3]
  • With aging, the cortical outer shell thins and the inner trabecular architecture becomes thinner and less connected; comparative cutaways are illustrative schematics, not diagnostic scans. [3]
  • Bone remodeling removes old bone and forms new bone; bone loss occurs when removal outpaces replacement. [3]
  • Osteoporosis is often silent until a fracture; hip, spine and wrist are common fracture sites, sometimes after minor trauma. [1] [3]
  • Vertebral compression fractures may shorten the front of a vertebral body, causing back pain, height loss and a stooped posture; not every spinal fracture is due to osteoporosis. [1] [4]
  • New severe back pain or pain after a minor fall warrants prompt clinical assessment for a possible fracture rather than self-diagnosis. [1] [4]
  • Risk factors include older age, estrogen decline after menopause, family history and long-term glucocorticoid use; men can also develop osteoporosis. [1] [3]
  • Evaluation combines fracture and medical history with bone-density testing; DXA measures bone mineral density, commonly at the hip and spine, and helps assess fracture risk. [2] [3]
  • Care may include adequate calcium, vitamin D and protein, individually appropriate weight-bearing and resistance exercise, balance work and home fall prevention. Exercise should be tailored to fracture risk. [2] [3]
  • Clinician-selected medicines can slow bone loss or build bone and reduce fracture risk; treatment is individualized, and nutrition alone is not a substitute for indicated medication. [2] [3]
