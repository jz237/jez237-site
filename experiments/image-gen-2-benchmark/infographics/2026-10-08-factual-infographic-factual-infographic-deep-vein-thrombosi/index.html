Deep Vein Thrombosis — Flow Meets a Clot

Deep-vein anatomy and restricted venous return, risk associations, warning signs, pulmonary embolism, medical evaluation and management, risk reduction and post-thrombotic valve injury.

Research behind the infographic

  • DVT is a blood clot in a deep vein, usually in the lower leg, thigh or pelvis; it can also occur in the arm. [1] [3]
  • Veins return blood from limbs and organs to the heart; a venous clot may restrict that return and cause pain and swelling. [2] [3]
  • Platelets and plasma proteins cooperate in clot formation; dangerous clots may form inside vessels without an obvious injury or fail to dissolve naturally. [2]
  • Slow flow from limited movement and injury to veins can increase DVT risk; surgery and major injury are associated with higher VTE risk. [1] [3]
  • Increased estrogen and family history can raise VTE risk; risk factors do not establish a diagnosis. [1]
  • DVT symptoms can include swelling, pain or tenderness, warmth, and redness or discoloration in the affected leg or arm; DVT can occur without symptoms. [1]
  • Possible DVT symptoms require prompt medical evaluation; special medical tests are needed to diagnose DVT or PE. [1]
  • A detached venous clot can travel through the heart to the lungs and block pulmonary blood flow, causing pulmonary embolism. [2] [3]
  • Difficulty breathing, chest pain that worsens with breathing or coughing, coughing blood, or fainting can be PE signs and require immediate medical help; PE can occur without DVT symptoms. [1]
  • Anticoagulants prevent a clot from growing or spreading and prevent new clots; clot-dissolving medicines or clot removal are used in selected situations rather than for everyone. [2]
  • Movement after confinement and leg exercises during long sitting can help reduce risk; individualized preventive medicines or compression stockings should be discussed with a clinician. [1]
  • Damage to vein valves after DVT can cause post-thrombotic syndrome with persistent swelling, pain, skin discoloration, or ulcers in severe cases. [1]
