Immaculate Antlion — The Funnel Trap Underfoot

A source-verified natural-history plate showing the larva’s anatomy, backward spiral excavation, conical pit mechanics, sand-flick prey capture, three larval instars, silk-and-soil cocoon and net-winged adult.

Research behind the infographic

  • Myrmeleon immaculatus is a valid species in the antlion family Myrmeleontidae and insect order Neuroptera. [1] [2]
  • The species is native to North America and Central America and is most often encountered in the United States east of the Mississippi River. [1]
  • Females place eggs in dry powdery or sandy soil, often in sheltered loose soil near foundations or tree bases where overhangs or canopy reduce flooding. [1]
  • The gray-brown, slightly hairy larva has armor-like plating, six legs and enlarged sickle-shaped mandibles. [1]
  • The larva moves backward through loose soil in a spiral and flips soil away with its head and mandibles to excavate a funnel-shaped pit. [1]
  • The larva waits buried at the pit bottom with its mandibles exposed while loose dry grains give small arthropod prey poor traction. [1]
  • When prey tries to climb out, rapid head-and-mandible flicks throw sand onto the slope and can create small landslides toward the larva. [1]
  • The larva seizes small arthropod prey with its mandibles, injects digestive secretions, extracts liquefied tissues and discards the remains outside the pit. [1]
  • Myrmeleon immaculatus passes through three larval instars and the final instar spins silk that binds soil particles into a cocoon before pupation. [1]
  • The adult is a nocturnal, slender-bodied insect about one inch long with net-veined, unspotted wings. [1]
  • Observed pits are roughly three-quarters to two inches wide and about one-half inch deep; wet soil impedes construction and rain can destroy pits. [1]
  • As a carnivorous sit-and-wait predator of ants and other small arthropods, the larva is part of forest and landscape food webs and does not harm nearby plants. [1]
