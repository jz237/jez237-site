Nautilus — The Living Buoyancy Spiral

A direct-canvas anatomy and physics infographic showing how ion transport, osmosis, chamber liquid, and gas change a chambered nautilus's overall density while a separate head siphon provides directional jet propulsion.

Research behind the infographic

  • Among living cephalopods, the nautilus has an external chambered shell, and the animal occupies the outermost living chamber. [1]
  • Older shell chambers are connected by a tube of living tissue called the siphuncle. [1] [2]
  • The siphuncle regulates the relative volumes of liquid and gas in the shell chambers, changing the animal's overall density and buoyancy. [1] [2]
  • Active ion transport creates osmotic gradients that move water between chamber liquid and siphuncular tissue. [2]
  • When ions and then water leave a chamber, its gas space expands; dissolved gases diffuse into the lower-pressure chamber and come out of solution, reducing overall density and increasing buoyancy. [2]
  • The reverse process increases chamber liquid, increases overall density, and reduces buoyancy. [2]
  • As a nautilus grows, it adds a new larger living chamber and leaves older chambers behind within the spiral shell. [1]
  • Buoyancy regulation sets lift or trim, while directional swimming is produced separately by jet propulsion through a siphon near the head. [1]
  • A simplified submarine-ballast comparison is useful, but the nautilus moves chamber liquid through living siphuncular tissue rather than opening a flood valve. [2]
