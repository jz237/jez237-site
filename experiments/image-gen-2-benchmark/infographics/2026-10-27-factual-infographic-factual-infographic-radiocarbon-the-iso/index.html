Radiocarbon — The Isotope Clock

An artwork-led journey from atmospheric carbon-14 production through photosynthesis to closed-sample decay and qualified radiocarbon age interpretation.

Research behind the infographic

  • Carbon-12 has six protons and six neutrons and is stable; carbon-14 has six protons and eight neutrons and is radioactive. [1]
  • Cosmic-ray cascades produce neutrons in the upper atmosphere; nitrogen-14 captures a neutron and emits a proton to form carbon-14. [3]
  • Atmospheric radiocarbon enters carbon dioxide, which plants take up by photosynthesis; animals acquire carbon through food. [2]
  • After a sample stops exchanging carbon with its surroundings, the radiocarbon to stable-carbon ratio decreases exponentially through radioactive decay. [2]
  • Carbon-14 decays to nitrogen. [3]
  • An ideal closed sample retains half its initial carbon-14 after each half-life; the relative sequence is 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, and the decay curve approaches zero rather than dropping linearly. [2]
  • Radiocarbon dating measures remaining radiocarbon relative to stable carbon to infer an age. [2]
  • Wood records the growth of the sampled tree ring, not necessarily when the wood was used. [2]
  • Atmospheric radiocarbon has varied, so calibration against material of known age is needed for calendar ages. [2] [3]
  • Contamination can introduce carbon of another age; chemical pretreatment aims to remove it. [2]
  • Marine reservoir effects can shift apparent ages because ocean carbon differs from atmospheric carbon. [2]
  • The method applies to once-living carbon-containing material; stone and metal usually cannot be dated directly by radiocarbon. [2]
