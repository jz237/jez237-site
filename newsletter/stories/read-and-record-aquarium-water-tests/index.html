Freshwater / Practical guide

Record aquarium test results with units and context

Use the kit's method the same way each time, and keep a record that shows whether a change came from the water or from how you tested.

The Aquarium Journal · 2 min read

Write down the result, its units and what was going on in the aquarium at the time. You can't compare “water fine” with next week's result. A good record shows the measurement and the circumstances, and is honest about how precise a color match can be.

Make the measurement repeatable

Read the instructions for each test in the kit. API tells users to mix designated reagent bottles vigorously and capped test tubes more gently, and to rinse tubes promptly after testing. Don't assume every bottle is handled the same way, and don't borrow another kit's sequence.2

Check expiry information and note when each reagent was opened. Measure the specified sample volume and time every required wait. Read the color with the instructed viewing arrangement in suitable light, because colored aquarium lighting skews the comparison. Contamination, incorrect quantities and reading at the wrong time can all distort the result.1

Use one line per observation

  • Tank name; date and time; whether the sample was aquarium water or replacement water.
  • Test name; kit or method; result and units exactly as displayed; any uncertainty in the color match.
  • Before or after a water change; recent filter work; changes in feeding or livestock; observations of fish behavior.
  • What you checked next, with the repeat result entered separately rather than overwriting the first.

An entry can say “between these two chart colors” instead of inventing an extra decimal place. If you switch kits, note the switch. Tests can use different scale intervals, so two readings won't always line up exactly. When they disagree substantially, check the method before trusting the more reassuring number.1

What to monitor

Monitor ammonia, nitrite and nitrate along with pH and temperature, and pay closer attention while a new tank is establishing. Testing tells you how filtration and husbandry are working, which clear-looking water can't.3

Judge pH against your species' requirements; there is no universal freshwater target. API also warns that ammonia and nitrite matter even at low levels. Look into an unexpected reading promptly. A tidy log is useful evidence, but keeping one is no reason to ignore a possible water-quality problem.2
