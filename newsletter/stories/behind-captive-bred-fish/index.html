Breeding marine fish requires the right live food for their larvae

Young fish need prey of the right size and nutritional value. Hatcheries raise food organisms alongside the fish.

The Aquarium Journal · 3 min read
Earlier research

A breeder can have eggs, clean water and attentive care and still be missing the meal that gets larvae to the next stage. For marine fish larvae, food has to fit a tiny mouth, trigger a hunting response and deliver the right nutrients.3

The University of Florida’s February 25, 2025 account of its ornamental-fish research comes back to this problem. UF teams had raised blue tangs in captivity, and that depended partly on learning to grow the live foods the larvae needed at different stages. The university described the goal as methods fish farmers could use, so that each producer would not have to solve the life cycle alone.1

How clownfish differ

Clownfish eggs stick to a surface, which in a breeding system can be a tile or clay pot, and the male tends the nest. The larvae hatch relatively well developed and can take enriched rotifers as their first food. Many pelagic spawners release buoyant eggs instead, and their less-developed larvae need smaller prey, so a routine that works for clownfish does not carry over to every species.2

There are exceptions. UF/IFAS notes that damselfishes also lay eggs on surfaces and provide parental care, yet their larvae often need small copepod nauplii. Where a fish lays its eggs doesn't tell a breeder everything about what will hatch; the larva’s capabilities matter as much as the parents’ behavior.2

Growing the food

Rotifers can be grown densely, and breeders enrich them to improve their nutritional value. Brine shrimp are convenient to hatch but can be too large for the earliest feeding stage. Copepod nauplii offer a different mix of size, movement and nutrition, though keeping up their supply brings its own culture challenges.3

That is why breeding a species once and producing it dependably are separate milestones. UF’s account describes the intensive labor and cost of developing larval diets and environments. A farmer needs a method that works batch after batch, and the research aims to make that stage less precarious.1

Questions to ask about the fish in front of you

Who bred this fish, and can its origin be documented? What is it eating here, and can I watch it feed? What adult size, social behavior and housing needs should I plan for? Ask about the individual fish being offered as well as the species on the label; a familiar appearance is no record of where an animal was born.

Captive breeding can offer an alternative to collecting fish, although UF’s account also acknowledges uncertainty about wild harvest’s effects. Our view is to ask for a traceable origin instead of turning that uncertainty into a blanket verdict on every wild fish. Choosing a fish's source is also not the same as protecting its habitat. Valuing a hatchery's work and caring about living reefs go together.1
