A pistol shrimp’s snap comes from a collapsing bubble, one of the loudest sounds in the sea

The claw shuts so fast that it shoots out a jet of water and a bubble. NOAA puts the bang at 183–210 decibels.

Aquarium News · November 1, 2026 · 1 min read
  • Shrimp & snails
Earlier research
Link: jez237.com/newsletter/stories/pistol-shrimp-snap-bubble/

Some of the loudest animals on a reef are a couple of centimeters long, under an inch. Snapping shrimp, also called pistol shrimp, are a group of species with one oversized claw that can grow to half the size of the body. NOAA’s sanctuary scientists put their snaps at 183 to 210 decibels, which ranks them among the loudest sound makers in the ocean.2

A team led by Michel Versluis filmed the claw of Alpheus heterochaelis at high speed while recording with a hydrophone, and reported in Science in 2000 that the sound comes from a bubble rather than the claw itself. The claw closes so fast that it fires a jet of water quick enough to make a cavitation bubble, and the snap is heard when that bubble collapses. The snap can stun or kill small prey.1

Together, their snaps make the crackle divers hear, which NOAA compares to milk poured on Rice Krispies. Its listening project found snapping varies by place, season and time of day, generally rising as water warms. Researchers now track it as a possible sign of how well a damaged habitat is recovering.2
